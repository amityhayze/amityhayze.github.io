Listening report // SR–001

Steve O Creep — Love and Death

Steve O Creep 22 tracks Album review Full Transmission

For this edition of Full Transmission, presented by Send the Track, I'm taking a look at Love and Death, a 22-track project by Steve O Creep that blends underground rap with alternative influences.

Rather than focusing on individual tracks alone, I wanted to take a closer look at the project as a whole, including the production, vocal performances, mixing, mastering, and the creative decisions behind the music. Sometimes the most interesting parts of an album are the things you notice after spending time with it (especially when you start thinking about how all the pieces fit together).

Steve does all the production himself, along with the mixing and mastering, and the project features contributions from artists including 85 Sicc, Doc Gruesome, Oddknot, DJ Kitty, Smokeydabear and Bea Creep.

01 / Production and Pacing

One of the first things that stood out to me was the pacing. With 22 tracks, there's a lot of room for a project to lose momentum, but I really enjoy when an album keeps me engaged by giving me something different and unique to focus on.

Love and Death has a lot of variety in its production, moving between melodic singing, rapping, and more aggressive vocal performances.

That variety helps the project feel like more than a collection of songs using the same formula. The changes in delivery, production choices, and vocal effects give individual tracks their own character. The layering and effects help distinguish the different approaches throughout the album. , and add a certain "ear candy" I enjoy!

As someone who produces, records, mixes, and masters my own music, (cough, cough "AMITY HAYZE" I always find it interesting to hear how another artists approache the entire process themselves. When one person is responsible for so many parts of a project, those decisions can become closely connected. Production choices affect how a vocal sits in the mix, and the way a vocal is performed can influence how the instrumental needs to support it.

My question to Steve Steve, how does being responsible for the entire process affect your sound? Do you think you approach mixing differently when you're also the person creating the instrumental?
Steve responds ARTIST RESPONSE 01

Great question. I absolutely do approach mixing and mastering differently, especially since this is my first time doing it. This is my first project I've ever handled myself. Generally, it's 85 Sicc that does it. He's my business partner, my best friend, my Twinfolk, my brother for real.

But he was unavailable for the foreseeable future, so I took the reins. I think it came through how I wanted it to. The parts that shine really shine, and it's because I have complete control over what I choose to make warmer, brighter, louder, quieter.

Producing myself has always been kind of a... I'm a stickler for, you know, doing that. I rarely work with other people on other beats unless it's like a feature or co-production. Simply because I come from a background of being in bands, and I think the music should be handled in-house.

02 / The Contrast Between Sound and Subject Matter

Something else that stood out to me was the contrast between the energy of the music and the subject matter.

Love and Death touches on heartbreak, regret, religion, death, wanting help, and forgiveness. Tracks like "Dear God," "Regret," and "Please Forgive Me Clark" really highlight those heavier themes for me.

I also really enjoy the tension between the production, which is constantly moving and evolving, and the subject matter, which is much darker and more vulnerable. The music doesn't always have to sound as heavy as the emotions being expressed. Sometimes that contrast makes the subject matter more interesting because the listener has to battle with what they're hearing sonically with what the artist is communicating.

I think this contrast gives the album room to move between different emotional states without relying on one sound to communicate everything. The variety in vocal delivery and production helps each track approach its subject matter differently.

My question to Steve Steve, how do those themes connect to the title Love and Death? Did you have that central idea in mind from the beginning, or did the meaning develop as you made the album?
Steve responds ARTIST RESPONSE 02

So for the themes of Love and Death, you know, love is the cousin of hate. Death is the brother of murder. It's poetic, it's beautiful, it's life, you know what I mean?

And this album, it was never... it wasn't laid out like "I'm gonna do 22 songs." It just was never done, until it was finished.

I told my wife three separate times that I was done with the album, and I kept lying. I was like, "It's not finished. It's not done. It doesn't feel right."

And when it happened, when "Death Pt. 1" and "Death Pt. 2" happened, it was like, "Oh, this is it. It's done."

There was so much going on in my life. I had just quit drinking, so there's still references to drinking halfway through the album. And along with that came the first draft of it being completed.

Then when I quit drinking, all these feelings came up and all this other shit happened in my life, and I had to speak on more of it.

03 / Favorite Tracks and Standout Moments

With 22 tracks, choosing favorites wasn't easy, but several songs stood out to me: "What If It's Me," "Pills on Her Tongue," "Crossing Over," "Dear God," "Regret," "Death Pt. 1," and "Death Pt. 2."

I also really love that final stretch between "Death Pt. 1" and "Death Pt. 2."

I think album sequencing can be an underrated part of making a project and I personally look for that when I listen to an album.Individual songs matter, obviously, but the order in which they appear can change how a listener interprets them and the whole project . When tracks share a title, theme, or musical idea, their placement can create a relationship that extends beyond either song on its own.

My question to Steve Steve, did you envision "Death Pt. 1" and "Death Pt. 2" as a connected ending? Is there a particular relationship between those tracks that listeners should pick up on?
Steve responds ARTIST RESPONSE 03

So "Death Pt. 1" and "Death Pt. 2," death was just a title track until I shuffled things around.

Like I had the song "Love" and the song "Death" planned for the album, obviously Love and Death, but it devolved into a partner. But in my first rendition of it, I was like, "I wanna go on a more personal note."

And it's a lot of video clips between me and my best friends, and just about life, you know. "What are we here for?" "Are we spiritually alive?" "Are we dead?" "What's going on?"

And the outro really... "Death Pt. 2" definitely hit home for me as the person making the music because it has acoustic guitar that I played in it.

And I'm a huge Jason Molina fan. Rest in peace. I regret never getting to see him live.

I really wanted to emulate that love for just poetic sadness that I have in the album, as the last note.

04 / Final Transmission

One of the things I appreciate about Love and Death is the range of approaches across the project. The production, vocal delivery, and subject matter give the album several different points of entry, while the recurring themes of heartbreak, regret, faith, and forgiveness give listeners something to follow across the 22 tracks.

Hearing Steve's responses added to how I understand the project. What initially stood out to me as a contrast between the energy of the production and the darker subject matter also connects to the circumstances surrounding the album's creation.

Steve described taking over the production, mixing, and mastering himself, and how the additional control allowed him to shape the sound according to his own decisions. He also explained that the album wasn't planned as a fixed 22-track project. Its ending came together as his life changed and he found more things he wanted to communicate.

The connection between "Death Pt. 1" and "Death Pt. 2" became particularly interesting with that context. What started as a title track eventually became the closing statement, with the acoustic guitar and outro reflecting the feeling Steve wanted to leave the listener with.

I'd like to thank Steve for sharing Love and Death with me, answering my questions, and giving me a closer look at the creative process behind it.

I'm Amity Hayze, and this has been Full Transmission, presented by Send the Track.

About Signal Report

Signal Report is part of Send the Track: Full Transmission, a series centered on listening to music and looking beyond the surface of a finished recording.

Some reports feature music selected independently. Others may be commissioned by artists. Each review reflects my own listening experience and perspective as a musician who writes, records, produces, mixes, and masters music.
